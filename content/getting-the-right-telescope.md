# Picking the right telescope
## How to choose the right telescope for your needs.

An initial spark of interest for a child starting out in astronomy can come from a variety of sources, including first-hand experience of spotting a bright object in the night sky such as Venus; a lesson at school or during an after-school activity; or something catching their imagination on television, YouTube, or other media platform. Whatever the source may be, one of the first steps a parent can take to address and encourage that curiosity is by asking the child what they would like to do, see, or discover next.


If there is a just a want to look at the night sky in more detail, a local astronomical society would be only too pleased to accommodate an accompanied child at an observing session. Seeing the craters of the Moon up close, the moons of Jupiter, or the rings of Saturn for the first time can be a thoroughly rewarding and unforgettable experience.


A pair of binoculars may also be a suitable option, as they can bring the night sky to life in so many ways, thereby proving a great first acquisition for any budding astronomer. A perfect starting pair for children would be a pair of 8 × 32 binoculars; anything larger can be difficult to hold and sometimes the interpupillary distances (the measurement between the centres of the pupils, critical for binoculars to deliver a clear, comfortable, and crisp viewing experience) cannot adjust to smaller heads. In addition, the ‘8×’ magnification is a bit more forgiving for shaky hands. The versatility and ease of use of binoculars may ultimately inspire a child to ask about possibly advancing to a telescope.


Whilst many consider a telescope as being essential in amateur astronomy, this isn’t necessarily the case, as much can be done with the naked eye or binoculars alone. This is why the adult must ensure that the step toward purchasing a telescope is the serious choice of the child. Once the passion for astronomy is recognized as genuine, the adult can then ensure that the craving for knowledge is adequately fed through the purchase of the *right* telescope and not just *any* telescope.


A type of telescope known as a [Dobsonian is a good first choice](https://www.firstlightoptics.com/beginner-telescopes.html). With their comparatively large apertures (the larger the aperture, the greater the ability to collect light from the object being viewed) fainter objects appear brighter, making them easier to observe. If the Moon is of specific interest, a straightforward refractor offers good quality optics and ease of use. Many Dobsonian telescopes are extremely large and difficult to move around but there are some excellent ‘tabletop’ Dobsonians now available which are very popular for children. ‘Tabletop’ telescopes are designed to sit on tables or flat surfaces, as opposed to the typical tripod-mounted telescopes.


Smartphones are very much on the minds of the young, and whereas parents may frown on their usage for some applications, this is an excellent opportunity to use smartphone technology to further the child’s interest in astronomy. Specific software installed on a phone can actually allow the user to instruct the telescope to automatically find and track objects in the night sky, such as with a GoTo mount, which literally ‘goes to’ the required object to look at in the night sky. Also, Celestron have their StarSense enabled telescopes that allow you to mount a smartphone on the telescope and use that as a PushTo system to find things – PushTo being similar to the GoTo system but much cheaper.


Part of the remit of Young Astronomers UK is to help young astronomers locate and purchase the most suitable telescope for their needs.
